Project Overview

Modeled and stabilized a nonlinear inverted pendulum system in Simulink, deriving the nonlinear equations of motion from first principles and implementing a PD controller for closed-loop upright stabilization. The system was then linearized around the upright equilibrium to analytically select controller gains, and validated across multiple initial angle conditions.

Design Methodology

01

Nonlinear Modeling

Derived the full nonlinear equations of motion for the inverted pendulum from Newton-Euler mechanics. Implemented the nonlinear plant model in Simulink using integrator blocks and signal routing.

02

Linearization

Linearized the nonlinear system around the upright equilibrium point (θ = 0) using small-angle approximations. The linearized transfer function enabled analytical gain selection using standard second-order system design methods.

03

PD Controller Design

Selected Kp and Kd gains by matching the closed-loop characteristic equation to a desired second-order response with damping ratio ζ = 0.7 and natural frequency ωn = 10 rad/s — targeting well-damped, fast settling behavior.

04

Derivative Noise Filter

Designed and added a lowpass filter on the derivative channel to attenuate high-frequency noise amplified by the D-term — a practical requirement for real controller implementations where sensor noise is present.

05

Validation Across Initial Conditions

Simulated the closed-loop system across four initial angles to assess the controller's effective region of attraction for the nonlinear plant.

Validation Results

The PD controller was tested across four initial pendulum angles. The nonlinear system's behavior diverges from the linearized model at larger angles, revealing the limits of linear design applied to a nonlinear plant.

The PD controller successfully stabilized the nonlinear inverted pendulum across all four tested initial conditions (5°, 30°, 35°, 50°).

Gain selection via linearization produced a well-damped closed-loop response (ζ=0.7, ωn=10 rad/s). The derivative lowpass filter suppressed noise amplification without significantly degrading transient performance.
